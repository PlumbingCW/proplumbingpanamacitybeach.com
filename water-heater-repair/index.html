Never touch a wet electric heater and never relight a gas pilot if you smell gas. A rotten egg smell means leave the house and call the gas utility from outside. A lit water heater in a closet or garage is not worth any risk.

Common Causes of a Failing Water Heater

Heaters fail in predictable ways. On electric units, the lower heating element and the thermostat are the usual suspects, and a tripped limit switch can look like a dead heater. On gas units, a dirty thermocouple, a weak pilot or a fouled burner can shut the flame down. A leak from the tank itself, as opposed to a fitting, is nearly always the end of the tank.

Sediment is a quiet cause. It builds at the bottom of the tank, insulates the water from the burner or element, and causes popping and rumbling. A flush can help a tank that still has life left. A tank past a decade of service with sediment and rust-colored water is more often a candidate for replacement.

Coastal Conditions That Wear Water Heaters Out

Salt air is the local factor. Heaters in garages, carports and outdoor closets near the Gulf and St. Andrew Bay are exposed to damp, salty air that corrodes jackets, fittings, venting and electrical connections faster than inland installs. Check the base of the unit for rust flaking and the relief valve for crusty deposits.

Rental homes add wear of their own. Back-to-back guests with long showers and laundry keep a tank cycling hard in summer, and a house left cold for weeks can leave sediment to harden. If you manage rentals near Laguna Beach, a yearly flush and valve check is a sensible habit.

How We Diagnose It

  • Confirm power or gas supply and reset safety controls
  • Test elements, thermostats and the limit switch on electric units
  • Check the thermocouple, pilot assembly and burner on gas units
  • Inspect the temperature and pressure relief valve and the discharge pipe
  • Look for the true leak source before calling the tank bad

Many leaks come from a fitting or the relief valve, not the tank, and those are inexpensive to fix. We trace the water before recommending a tank.

Repair or Replace?

A fair rule is that a heater under about eight to ten years old with a single failed part is usually worth repairing. A heater near or past that age with a leaking tank, rust or repeated repairs is usually better replaced. Age, brand, access and the cost of the part all decide.

If a new unit makes sense, see our water heater installation page for what a proper install includes, including permit and venting steps.

Tank Versus Tankless Repairs

Tankless units have different failure modes. Scale on the heat exchanger, a fouled flame sensor and flow sensor faults are common, and many error codes clear after descaling. We work on common tankless brands, and the tankless water heater page covers service and install options.
